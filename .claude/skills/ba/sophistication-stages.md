# The 5 Stages of Market Sophistication

> "How many similar products have they been told about before?"
> — Schwartz, Ch. 3 (Question 3 of the three diagnostic questions)

Sophistication measures **how much advertising your prospect has already been exposed to** in this category. It is independent of awareness. A market can know exactly what it wants (Solution Aware) AND have heard every possible variation of the claim for ten years (Stage 5). Your angle has to respect both axes.

| Stage | State of the market | Winning strategy |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | First — no similar products, no claims heard | **Name the claim bluntly.** Be simple, direct, unadorned. |
| 2 | Second — direct claim still working but competition arrives | **Enlarge the claim.** Push the promise to the limit of legality and believability. |
| 3 | Third — all the claims have been heard, prospects are numb | **Introduce a new mechanism.** Shift emphasis from *what* to *how*. |
| 4 | Fourth — mechanisms are now being imitated and elaborated | **Elaborate the mechanism.** Make it easier, faster, surer. |
| 5 | Fifth — market is exhausted, no one believes anything | **Identification.** Stop selling the claim or mechanism. Echo who the prospect *is*. |

---

## Stage 1 — You Are First

> "If you are the first in your particular market, with your particular product — then you are dealing with prospects that have no sophistication about your product at all… Your story is brand-new to them."

**Strategy:** *"Be simple. Be direct. Above all, don't be fancy. Name either the need or the claim in your headline — nothing more. Dramatize that claim in your copy — make it as powerful as possible. And then bring in your product; and prove that it works."*

**Classic example:** *"NOW! LOSE UGLY FAT!"* — the first reducing-field headline. All it had to do was state the direct desire of millions of women.

**When you're in Stage 1 by default:** technological breakthroughs (women's hair sprays when new), radically better products (long-playing records), familiar products at an explosively new price (Model T), or a newly-discovered use for an existing product (Ovaltine switched from insomnia aid to body builder for skinny children).

**Rule:** don't over-write. Nothing more is needed because nothing more is needed.

---

## Stage 2 — Second in the Market

> "If you're second, and the direct claim is still working — then copy that successful claim — but enlarge on it. Drive it to the absolute limit. Outbid your competition."

**Examples of enlargement, pushed to the edge:**
- *"LOSE UP TO 47 POUNDS IN 4 WEEKS — OR RECEIVE $40 BACK!"*
- *"I AM 61 POUNDS LIGHTER… NEVER A HUNGRY MINUTE."*
- *"PICK 25—50—100 ROSES FROM THIS ONE MAGNIFICENT PLANT!"*
- *"SIX HUNDRED MUMS FROM A SINGLE BUSH!"*
- *"WHO EVER HEARD OF 17,000 BLOOMS FROM A SINGLE PLANT?"*

**Strategy:** take the winning claim and make it bigger. More pounds, more blooms, more horsepower, more speed, more cleaning power, whiter-than-whites.

**What kills Stage 2:** the process "disintegrates. The successful claim is overworked; enlargement piles on enlargement. New competitors enter the field — each trying to promise more. Headlines double and triple in size. Words begin to lose their meaning — 'whiter-than-whites' appear. The prospect becomes confused — then skeptical. Believability is shattered; claims are automatically discounted 50% by their readers."

When you see skepticism setting in and sales curves bending down, you've exited Stage 2.

---

## Stage 3 — The Promise Is Old; The Mechanism Is New

> "If your market is at the stage where they've heard all the claims, in all their extremes, then mere repetition or exaggeration won't work any longer. What this market needs now is a **new device** to make all these old claims become fresh and believable to them again. In other words, A NEW MECHANISM — a new way to making the old promise work."

**Critical shift:** *emphasis moves from WHAT the product does to HOW it works.* The claim itself stays the same — "lose weight," "stop smoking," "grow your business." But the mechanism that accomplishes it is new.

**Examples in the reducing field:**
- Old Stage 2: *"I AM 61 POUNDS LIGHTER… NEVER A HUNGRY MINUTE."* (just the claim)
- New Stage 3: *"FLOATS FAT RIGHT OUT OF YOUR BODY!"* (mechanism in the headline)
- Also Stage 3: *"FIRST WONDER DRUG FOR REDUCING!"* (mechanism = wonder drug)

The headline now explains the mechanism; the claim itself may be restated below it, in the lead paragraph, to confirm what the prospect is getting.

**Rule for Stage 3:** lead with mechanism. Let the old claim sit underneath as confirmation. The new mechanism is your point of difference — the thing that makes the stale promise fresh again.

**Warning:** the mechanism you introduce must be "accepted as believable and significant by your market." A new mechanism that feels arbitrary, gimmicky, or too technical to evaluate will not break through.

---

## Stage 4 — Elaborate the Mechanism

> "You are still in a competitive market, and such ads give only a temporary advantage. Such ads, presenting a new promise, begin a new trend. Within a few months, the Third Stage of Sophistication passes into a Fourth Stage — a new stage of elaboration and enlargement. But this time, the elaboration is concentrated on the mechanism, rather than on the promise."

**Example:**
- Stage 3: *"FIRST WONDER DRUG FOR REDUCING!"*
- Stage 4: *"FIRST NO-DIET REDUCING WONDER DRUG!"*

**Strategy (Schwartz's exact words):** *"If a competitor has just introduced a new mechanism to achieve the same claim as that performed by your product, and that new mechanism announcement is producing sales, then you counter him in this way. Simply elaborate or enlarge upon the successful mechanism. Make it easier, quicker, surer; allow it to solve more of the problem; overcome old limitations; promise extra benefits."*

This stage resembles Stage 2 — it's an enlargement game — but applied to the *mechanism* rather than the *claim*. The same limits will eventually be reached: prospects stop believing the mechanism, the market saturates again, and you must either discover a new mechanism or move to Stage 5.

---

## Stage 5 — Identification (The "Dead" Market)

> "In this Final Stage of Sophistication, your market no longer believes in your advertising, and therefore no longer wishes to be aware of your product… this Fifth Stage of Sophistication corresponds to the Fifth Stage of Awareness discussed in Chapter 2. The problems are the same. The strategy is the same. The emphasis shifts from the promise and the mechanism which accomplishes it, to **identification with the prospect himself**. You are dealing here with the problem of bringing your prospect into your ad — not through desire — but through identification."

**Example:** Postum's *"WHY MEN CRACK…"* ad (also used as the classic identification headline in awareness Level 5).

When Stage 5 hits, you stop trying to sell *the product*. You sell *who the user of the product is*. You mirror their attitude, their emotion, their self-image. The product almost disappears from the ad.

**Modern Stage 5 signals:**
- Every competitor is running mechanism-heavy ads that all blur together.
- Review sites are full of "they're all the same."
- CTRs are flat and CPMs are rising.
- Prospects actively avoid ads in this category.

**What to do:** Switch to identification copy. Make the ad about the prospect, not the product. The Marlboro virile-man campaign is the archetype.

## The Cigarette Industry: A Full Run Through All Five Stages

Schwartz's canonical worked example of an industry cycling through all 5 stages:

| Stage | Era | Headline | What happened |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Early market | *"I'D WALK A MILE FOR A CAMEL!"* / *"CHESTERFIELD — THEY SATISFY!"* | Raw promise of enjoyment |
| 2 | Enlargement | *"LIGHT UP A LUCKY, AND YOU WON'T MISS THE SWEETS THAT MAKE YOU FAT!"* | Broader comparisons (can't measure pleasure, so compare) |
| 3 | Mechanism | *"LUCKIES — THEY'RE TOASTED!"* / *"PALL MALL'S GREATER LENGTH FILTERS THE SMOKE FURTHER!"* / *"CAMELS — PROTECT YOUR T-ZONE!"* | New mechanisms emerge |
| 4 | Mechanism elaboration | *"PHILIP MORRIS — ALL THE HARSHNESS BAKED OUT!"* / *"CHESTERFIELD — REGULAR AND KING-SIZED TOO!"* / *"NINE OUT OF TEN DOCTORS PREFER LUCKIES!"* | Mechanisms imitated and enlarged |
| 5 | Identification | Marlboro virile men (cowboys, racing drivers, sky divers) — no words | Pure projection of the smoker's self-image |

When filter cigarettes opened up as a *new* product category, they retraced the same 5 stages starting from Stage 1 again (*"KENT'S MICRONITE FILTER TRAPS TARS BEFORE THEY REACH YOUR LIPS"*).

**Lesson:** the cycle is not linear across industries. Every new product, new mechanism, new breakthrough opens a fresh Stage 1 — even inside a category that is globally at Stage 4 or 5. Your job is to **find the unit of the market that is still at Stage 1 for your specific angle.**

## The "Restorative Power" of a Market

A key Schwartz insight, easy to miss:

> "One factor is vital here. That is the **restorative power** of the market you are dealing with… the desire never fades; the market continually renews itself. New prospects come into the market. Old customers become dissatisfied with their old purchases, their old solutions, and begin to look again. The mass desire — the tremendous profit potential — still exists. *But it cannot be tapped by the old, simple methods any longer.*"

This is why apparently "dead" markets keep producing winners. The desire is permanent; the claims go stale. A new copywriter enters, spots the restorative current, brings a new mechanism or a new identification — and the market rewards them.

**Practical consequence:** never write off a category as exhausted without asking: *has the desire died, or have the claims died?* Almost always, it's the claims.

---

## How to locate your market's sophistication

1. **Pull 5–10 competitor ads / landing pages / emails in your category.** Actually read them.
2. Is the dominant tactic (a) a bare claim, (b) an enlarged claim, (c) a mechanism, (d) an elaborated mechanism, (e) identification / emotional projection?
3. That's your stage. Your job is to write one stage ahead.

**Stage arithmetic:**
- Everyone else is at Stage 2 → you go to Stage 3 (mechanism).
- Everyone else is at Stage 3 → you go to Stage 4 (mechanism elaboration) or skip to Stage 5 (identification).
- Everyone else is at Stage 5 → look for a new mechanism that has become believable again and reset the cycle. Or find a splinter market still at Stage 1 for your angle.

The wrong move is to write at the same stage as the competition. That's how you become an echo ad.
