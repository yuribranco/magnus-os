# Mass Desire — The Force That Makes Advertising Work

## The core claim

> "The power, the force, the overwhelming urge to own that makes advertising work, comes from the market itself, and not from the copy. Copy cannot create desire for a product. It can only take the hopes, dreams, fears and desires that already exist in the hearts of millions of people, and focus those already-existing desires onto a particular product."
> — Schwartz, Ch. 1

If you remember nothing else from Breakthrough Advertising, remember this. **Copy is a direction tool, not an origination tool.** Every tactic in this skill assumes you are channeling an existing mass desire, not trying to invent one.

## Definition

> "It is the public spread of a private want. It is the moment when a private desire is shared by a statistically significant number of people, large enough to profitably repay selling these people, that a market is born."

Mass desire is **not** what one person wants. It's the moment when *enough* people want the same thing that an advertiser can reach them economically. This is a statistical phenomenon, not a psychological one.

## The Amplification Effect

> "It is this fact, when used correctly, that gives advertising its enormous potential for profit. By simply *directing* this gigantic, already-existing mass desire — rather than being required to create it — advertising thus commands an economic force hundreds of times more powerful than the mere number of dollars that the advertiser can spend on it. This is the Amplification Effect of successful advertising: the reason that $1 spent on such advertising can create $50 or even $100 in sales."

**Corollary:** when you try to *create* desire instead of channel it, you lose the amplification effect. Schwartz: *"When it tries to create this desire, it is no longer advertising but education. And, as education, it can produce at best only one dollar in sales for every dollar spent."*

Any copy brief that asks you to "educate the market" or "change people's minds" is a red flag. Either reframe the brief to surface an existing desire the product can channel, or walk away.

## Why fighting mass desire kills copy (Schwartz's three cases)

Schwartz opens Chapter 1 with three automotive cases that demonstrate the principle:

1. **Chrysler (1948)** — the public wanted longer, lower, wider cars. Chrysler offered a functional car — more interior room, "but shorter and squatter on the outside." A multi-million-dollar campaign by one of America's best agencies. Result: *catastrophic.*
2. **Ford (1954)** — horsepower was the winning appeal. Ford chose to push *safety* instead. The ads "ran into a wall of disinterest." The next year, Ford produced the highest-horsepower engines in its history.
3. **The Edsel** — a fine car, heavily advertised, which "died trying to fight the overwhelming switch in demand to a cheap, simple, inexpensive-to-run compact car."

**The pattern:** each product ran *against* the current of mass desire. In each case, the advertiser had more money and better creative resources than a normal campaign. None of it mattered. *You cannot create mass desire, and you cannot fight it. But you can — and must — direct it, channel it, focus it onto your particular product.*

## The two categories of mass desire

### 1. Permanent Forces

Desires that never fade. The copywriter's job here is **differentiation**, not trend-spotting.

- **Mass instinct** — the desire of women to be attractive, men to be virile, everyone to be healthy. The instinct never fades; the problem is not picking the trend (it's obvious) but shifting desire from a competitor's fulfillment to yours. Build a stronger claim, a fresher appeal, a new believability.
- **Mass technological problem** — an unsolved irritant: bad TV reception, corroding mufflers, how long aspirin takes to work. Until the problem is finally solved, prospects will buy and try, buy and try. Same claim as competitors, but offered in a new way.

### 2. Forces of Change

Desires that rise, peak, and reverse. The copywriter's job here is **timing**.

- **The beginning, fulfillment, and reversal of a trend.** Style. The sudden mass decision to install a pool instead of buying a bigger car. The shift from horsepower to gas economy. Here the copywriter needs sensitivity, foresight, intuition — the ability to catch the rising tide when it's still imperceptible. And, always, "how to be there first."
- **Mass education.** The slow trickle-down from celebrities, TV, politicians, movies, community pioneers, and "the sum total of all advertising" to the mass population. Timing question: when does the shift become statistically significant? When do enough people make the change to justify the campaign?

> "The copy writer is faced with a society containing dozens — even hundreds — of these already-existing mass desires. His first job therefore is to detect them — inventory them — chart their force and direction. This is a study that will occupy part of every working day for the rest of his life."

## The three dimensions of any mass desire

Every mass desire can be measured on three axes. Use them to rank which desire to tap.

| Dimension | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Urgency** | Intensity, degree of demand to be satisfied | Chronic arthritic pain > minor headache |
| **Staying power** | Repetition, inability to become satiated | Raw hunger > gourmet craving |
| **Scope** | Number of people who share this desire | Men who'll pay $10 for a gas-saver >> men who'll pay $10 for a repair-bill-preventer |

> "Every product appeals to two, three or four of these mass desires. But only one can predominate; only one can reach out through your headline to your customer. Only one is the key that unlocks the maximum economic power at the particular time your advertisement is published. **Your choice among these alternate desires is the most important step you will take in writing your ad.** If it is wrong, nothing else that you do in the ad will matter."

Every product has multiple desires it could appeal to. You choose **one**, and everything else subordinates to it. This is the *Concentration* technique (see the 7 body copy techniques in SKILL.md).

## The three tools the copywriter uses

1. **Knowledge of the prospect's hopes, dreams, desires, and emotions** (the mass desires).
2. **The client's product** (what it does, its mechanisms, its personality).
3. **The advertising message** that connects them.

And the copywriter does the work in three stages:

1. **Choose the most powerful desire** (using the three dimensions above).
2. **Acknowledge that desire — reinforce it — and/or offer the means to satisfy it — in a single statement in the headline.** This is where awareness level matters: your headline starts with the product (Level 1–2), the desire (Level 3), or the problem (Level 4), or with identification (Level 5).
3. **Take the series of performances built into your product — what your product does — and show your prospect how these product performances inevitably satisfy that desire.** This is the body copy.

## Physical product vs. functional product

> "In reality, every product you are given to sell is actually two products. One of them is the *physical* product — the steel, glass, paper or tobacco that the manufacturer has shaped into a particular pattern, of which he is justly proud. The other is the *functional* product — the product in action — the series of benefits that your product performs for your consumer, and on the basis of which he buys your product."

**No physical part of your product can ever become a headline.** No one will buy the size of the plant, the weight of the steel, the care of the construction. These physical facts only document and reinforce the primary performance that you promise in your headline.

**Physical facts are used to:**
- Justify your price ("the more tubes in the set, the more dollars it can command — *if* it first delivers the performance the prospect demands")
- Document the quality of performance
- Assure the performance will continue over time
- Sharpen the reader's mental picture of the performance
- Give an old claim a new basis for believability (introduce a new mechanism or limitation removed)

## The performance menu

An automobile, to use Schwartz's example, offers all of these at once:

- **Transportation** — carrying yourself, family, luggage, pets, furniture
- **Dependability** — freedom from breakdown, stalling, embarrassment
- **Economy** — savings on gas, oil, repairs; resale value; insurance
- **Power** — horsepower, acceleration, top speed
- **Recognition** — status, admiration, the smell of a new car
- **Value** — feet of steel per dollar, long life, high trade-in
- **Novelty** — power steering, iridescent paint, being the pioneer

Every product contains a menu like this. Your job: **list every performance, cross them against the existing mass desires in your market, and feature the ONE performance that will harness the greatest sales power right now.** Not all at once. One.

> "Every product gives you dozens of keys. But only one will fit the lock. Your job is to find that one dominant performance, squeeze every drop of power out of it in your presentation — and then convince your reader that that performance and that satisfaction can come only from your product."

## The single-thought rule

> "Your headline is limited by physical space. You have only one glance of the reader's eye to stop him. He is preoccupied; he is not looking for your product or your message — the span of his attention will admit only one thought to penetrate his indifference during that glance. **If your first thought holds him, he will read the second. If the second holds him, he will read the third. And if the third thought holds him, he will probably read through your ad.**"

This is the discipline behind every headline decision in this skill. **One thought. Not two.** You get one shot to stop a stranger.
