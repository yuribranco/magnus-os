# The 7 Breakthrough Copy Techniques — Body Copy Quick Reference

Part I of Breakthrough Advertising (awareness, sophistication, 38 operations) decides **what to say in your headline**. Part II — the 7 techniques — decides **how to build body copy that converts the reader the headline stopped**.

> "From that moment on, your body copy does the selling. It does this by altering your prospect's vision of reality. It creates a new world for your prospect — a world in which your product emerges as the fulfillment of the dominant desire that caused this man to respond to your headline."
> — Schwartz, Part II, Ch. 6

The body copy has to expand or alter one or more of the three dimensions of the prospect's mental world:

1. **Desires** — wants, needs, cravings. Physical, material, sensual. Already exist. Your job: expand, sharpen, channel, give them a goal.
2. **Identifications** — roles the prospect wants to play, personality traits they want the product to help build or project. Status, belonging, self-image.
3. **Beliefs** — opinions, attitudes, prejudices, fragments of knowledge the prospect lives by. The emotionalized reason that filters new information.

> "Advertising, like science, must accept reality as it exists, not as it might wish it to exist. Only then can it alter reality — not by smashing into it head-on — but by exploiting its tendencies and giving direction to its energies."
> — Schwartz, Ch. 6

The 7 techniques are mechanisms for altering those three dimensions.

---

## 1. Intensification — sharpen and multiply the desire

**The core job.** Take the vague, amorphous desire that caused the reader to click and **translate it into one vivid scene of fulfillment after another**. Add concrete satisfaction after concrete satisfaction to the basic drive.

> "Advertising is the literature of desire … It gives form and content to desire. It provides it with a goal. These desires, as they exist in the mind of your prospect today, are indistinct. They are blurs — hazy, ambiguous, not yet crystallized into words or images. In most cases, they are simply vague emotions, without compulsion or direction. And as such, they have only a fraction of their true potential power."
> — Schwartz, Ch. 7

**What it looks like in copy:** detailed before/after, sensory descriptions, "imagine yourself…" scenes, a parade of concrete results (not "lose weight" but "pick up your daughter without your back screaming, fit into jeans you haven't worn since college, look in the mirror and not flinch").

**Two compression techniques** for limited space (catalogs, TV, radio, ads with tight word limits):
- **Compression** — boil projections and images down to a few key words.
- **Campaign** — repeat those key words across multiple ads, with progressive embellishments.

**Common mistake:** generic benefit lists. "Fast. Easy. Affordable." Those are labels, not intensification. Intensification requires you to put the reader IN the fulfillment, sensorially.

**When to reach for it:** every ad. This is the dominant technique when desire is the lever.

---

## 2. Identification — let the prospect become the person the product makes them

**The core job.** Give the reader a role to play. Not "you'll lose weight" but "you'll be the woman who walks into a room and people notice." Not "you'll make more money" but "you'll be the kind of man who provides for his family without apology."

> "These longings for identification — longings for a sharply-defined personality — longings for social status — are, of course, not material or physical or sensual at all. They complement and intensify the physical desires — add another dimension to them — by making each purchase serve a double duty. Thus, not only does a woman buy a low-calorie food to become thin, but in so doing she also builds again a radiant, attractive, youthful personality."
> — Schwartz, Ch. 8

**What it looks like in copy:** describing the people who already use the product (aspirational peer group), giving the reader language to describe their new self, showing the reactions of others to the new self.

**When to reach for it:** sophistication Stage 5 (identification is the primary strategy for exhausted markets), status-heavy products, lifestyle products, anything where social signaling is part of the purchase.

**Classic example:** the Marlboro virile-men campaign. No words about virility. The image IS the identification.

---

## 3. Gradualization — walk the reader from their current state to the purchase in steps so small each feels inevitable

**The core job.** Close the gap between where the prospect is now (skeptical, uncommitted, comfortable in inaction) and where you need them to be (convinced, committed, reaching for their wallet). You close it one small logical/emotional step at a time.

Schwartz's principle: every sentence must compel the next. If you force a leap the reader isn't ready for, you lose them — and they won't come back.

**What it looks like in copy:** problem → acknowledgment ("you're right to be skeptical") → partial reveal → proof → bigger reveal → mechanism → objection addressed → offer. Each step anticipates and dissolves the next objection.

**Common mistake:** jumping from "here's your problem" straight to "here's the product." The reader hasn't earned enough trust yet to accept the solution.

**When to reach for it:** long-form sales letters, VSLs, cold traffic, high-ticket offers, products that require belief change.

---

## 4. Redefinition — reframe the language, the beliefs, or the category so objections dissolve

**The core job.** The prospect has existing beliefs that block the sale. You cannot argue with them head-on. You reframe them.

> "Believing is a process — a process of fitting new facts into certain established patterns of thought and conviction. People believe in certain ways. These beliefs form a filter through which your product-information must pass or be rejected. And their already-established patterns of reasoning create habit-channels along which your copy must build its conviction — or die. You start with these beliefs as a base. You build up from them by using his kind of logic, not your own."
> — Schwartz, Ch. 6

**What redefinition does:** changes what a word means, what a category is, what the problem really is, what "success" looks like in this context. Persimmon's VSL does this when it redefines "the problem" from "I need better training" to "I need a functional practice" — and redefines "Persimmon" from "a training company" to "an infrastructure company."

**What it looks like in copy:** "You think X means Y. It actually means Z." "This isn't really about A, it's about B." "The problem was never your effort. The problem was the system."

**When to reach for it:** when the prospect's existing category/belief set blocks the sale. When competitors have defined the category in a way that favors them.

---

## 5. Mechanization — prove the claim by showing the reader HOW it works

**The core job.** Turn "lose weight" into "floats fat right out of your body." Turn "whiter wash" into "six-enzyme formula that releases trapped dirt molecules." The mechanism is the believability engine.

**What it looks like in copy:** a specific, concrete, inspectable explanation of how the result happens. Diagrams, named components, step-by-step, cause-and-effect chains.

**Why it matters:** in sophistication Stage 3+, the claim itself is no longer enough. The market has heard the claim. What it hasn't heard is a believable mechanism that makes the claim fresh again.

**Rule:** the mechanism must be **accepted as believable and significant** by your market. A mechanism that sounds arbitrary, too complex to evaluate, or gimmicky won't break through. Ground it in something the prospect can verify or imagine verifying.

**When to reach for it:** Stage 3–4 sophistication markets. Supplement, health, financial, and "new method" categories where the promise is old and the mechanism is the differentiator.

---

## 6. Concentration — subordinate every element to one dominant theme

**The core job.** Every ad has one theme. Everything else — every paragraph, every illustration, every proof element, every CTA variation — reinforces that one theme. Nothing else is allowed to compete for the reader's attention.

> "Every product gives you dozens of keys. But only one will fit the lock. Your job is to find that one dominant performance, squeeze every drop of power out of it in your presentation — and then convince your reader that that performance and that satisfaction can come only from your product."
> — Schwartz, Ch. 1

**Sign of a concentrated ad:** you can state the single theme in one sentence, and every paragraph traces back to it. You cannot cut any paragraph without weakening the theme.

**Sign of a diluted ad:** two or more themes compete. "It's fast AND safe AND affordable AND eco-friendly." The reader remembers none of them.

**When to reach for it:** every ad. Concentration is the editorial discipline that makes the other techniques work.

---

## 7. Camouflage — hide the sell behind something the prospect actually wants to read

**The core job.** A cold reader, scrolling on their phone, will not read an ad they recognize as an ad. But they will read a story. A guide. A news item. A warning. A case study. A confession. A list of mistakes.

**What it looks like in copy:** the ad is dressed up as content. A headline like *"Why Men Crack…"* reads like a newspaper story, not a coffee-substitute sales letter. *"Do YOU make these mistakes in English?"* reads like self-improvement education, not a correspondence-course ad. *"The amazing story of a Zippo that worked after being taken from the belly of a fish"* reads like a tall tale, not a lighter ad.

**Why it matters:** advertising resistance is real and rising. The prospect's first response to any ad is suspicion. Camouflage lets the message past the guard.

**Rule:** the content the ad is disguised as must be **genuinely valuable or interesting** on its own. Fake camouflage — content that's obviously a sell in disguise — makes the prospect more suspicious, not less.

**When to reach for it:** cold traffic, Meta/TikTok feed ads, editorial-style landing pages, advertorials, email nurture sequences. Any context where the prospect did not ask to be sold to.

---

## How the 7 techniques relate to each other

They are not parallel alternatives. They layer:

- **Concentration** is the frame: one theme, chosen from the mass desire.
- **Intensification** and **Identification** are the emotional engines: they expand the desire and attach it to a self-image.
- **Gradualization** is the structural backbone: it sequences the copy so the reader never has to leap.
- **Redefinition** and **Mechanization** are the belief-changing tools: they make the claim fit through the filter and emerge believable.
- **Camouflage** is the outer layer: it gets the whole thing read in the first place.

A great long-form ad uses all seven. A great short ad (banner, subject line, hook) is dominated by whichever one or two the format allows.

## When body copy is failing, diagnose which technique is missing

| Symptom | Missing technique |
|---|---|
| Reader bounces at paragraph 2 | Camouflage (they recognized the ad) or Intensification too weak (first scene not vivid enough) |
| Reader reads but doesn't feel anything | Intensification (abstract benefits instead of concrete scenes) |
| Reader feels it but doesn't see themselves in it | Identification (no role to step into) |
| Reader is interested but skeptical of the claim | Mechanization (no believable how) |
| Reader agrees with the claim but can't bridge to the buy | Gradualization (too big a leap) |
| Reader's existing beliefs keep them from the sale even though they want it | Redefinition (needed to reframe) |
| Reader remembers "it was good" but can't say what it was about | Concentration (multiple competing themes) |

This table is the fastest way to pick a technique when body copy isn't landing.
