# The 5 States of Awareness

> "In its natural development, every market's awareness passes through several stages. The more aware your market, the easier the selling job, the less you need to say."
> — Schwartz, Ch. 2

Awareness is **orthogonal to sophistication**. A market can be highly aware (they know your product) and highly sophisticated (they've seen every claim). Diagnose both separately.

The scale runs from easiest to hardest:

| Level | Name | What the prospect knows | Headline job |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Most Aware | Knows product, knows it works, hasn't bought yet | Name + price/offer |
| 2 | Product Aware | Knows product exists, not fully convinced it's the right one | Product + superiority (1 of 7 sub-tasks) |
| 3 | Solution Aware | Knows they want the outcome, doesn't know a product exists for it | Crystallize the desire/solution |
| 4 | Problem Aware | Feels a problem or need, doesn't know solution exists | Name and dramatize the problem |
| 5 | Completely Unaware | Doesn't admit the problem, or it's diffuse/taboo | Identification — tell them who they are |

---

## Level 1 — Most Aware

> "The customer knows of your product—knows what it does—knows he wants it. At this point, he just hasn't gotten around to buying it yet. Your headline—in fact, your entire ad—need state little more except the name of your product and a bargain price."
> — Schwartz, Ch. 2

**Example:** *"Revere Zomar Lens, Electric Eye Camera — Formerly $149.50 — Now Only $119.95."*

**Typical contexts:** department store, discount store, mail-order bargain catalog, retargeting to warm cart abandoners, price-drop notifications to subscribers.

**Creative content:** near zero. This is a merchandise manager's job, not a copywriter's. The price is the headline.

---

## Level 2 — Product Aware (Knows Of It, Doesn't Yet Want It)

> "Here, your prospect isn't completely aware of all your product does, or isn't convinced of how well it does it, or hasn't yet been told how much better it does it now."
> — Schwartz, Ch. 2

This is where **the great bulk of all advertising** lives. You're dealing with a known product name, a known category, and a known desire. Your job is one of **seven sub-tasks**:

| Sub-task | Job | Schwartz's example |
|---|---|---|
| a | **Reinforce** existing desire for your product | Association — *"Steinway — The Instrument of the Immortals"* |
| b | **Sharpen** the image of how it satisfies desire | Sensory sharpening — *"Tastes like you just picked it — Dole"* |
| c | **Extend** where/when it satisfies desire | *"Thirst knows no season — Coca-Cola"* (winter ad) |
| d | **Introduce new proof/details** of satisfaction | *"9 out of 10 screen stars use Lux Toilet Soap"* |
| e | **Announce a new mechanism** to satisfy desire better | *"Hoover's new invention washes floors and vacuums up the scrub water"* |
| f | **Announce a new mechanism** that eliminates former limitations | *"A new Zenith hearing aid — inconspicuous beyond belief"* |
| g | **Change the image or mechanism** to escape competitive parity | (Sophistication problem — see sophistication-stages.md) |

**Rule for Level 2:** display the product name (headline or logo) and use the rest of the headline to point out its superiority. The body elaborates: visualization + documentation + mechanization.

---

## Level 3 — Solution Aware (Knows the Outcome, Not Your Product)

> "The prospect either knows, or recognizes immediately, that he wants what the product does; but he doesn't yet know that there is a product—your product—that will do it for him."
> — Schwartz, Ch. 2

**Two-fold problem:** (1) pinpoint the ill-defined desire that's slowly spreading through a population, (2) crystallize it sharply and dramatically so prospects recognize it at a glance.

**Three steps:**
1. Name the desire and/or its solution in the headline.
2. Prove the solution can be accomplished.
3. Show the mechanism of that accomplishment is in your product.

**Examples of crystallizing amorphous desire:**
- *"Light a Lucky, and you'll never miss sweets that make you fat."*
- *"Who else wants a whiter wash — with no hard work?"*
- *"How to win friends and influence people."*
- *"To men who want to quit work some day."*
- *"When doctors feel rotten — this is what they do."*
- *"Now! Run your car without spark plugs."*
- *"Who ever heard of 17,000 blooms from a single plant?"*

At Level 3 and below, **execution matters more than mechanics**. This is the domain of the idea man. Attributes required: analysis, intuition, verbal creativity.

**Rule for Level 3:** headline starts with the *desire*, not the product. The product is revealed later.

---

## Level 4 — Problem Aware (Has a Need, Not a Named Desire)

> "The prospect has — not a desire — but a need. He recognizes the need immediately. But he doesn't yet realize the connection between the fulfillment of that need and your product."
> — Schwartz, Ch. 2

This is the **problem-solving ad**. A special case of Level 3, treated the same way: name the need/solution in the headline, dramatize the need, present your product as the inevitable solution.

**Examples, from naked statement to verbal twist:**
- *"Corns?"* (just the problem)
- *"Stops maddening itch."* (problem + solution)
- *"Shrinks hemorrhoids without surgery."* (problem + solution + limitation removed)
- *"Now! A ring and piston job in a tube!"* (substitute for unpleasant task)
- *"Look, Mom! No cavities!"* (prevention of future problem)
- *"Do YOU make these mistakes in English?"* (emotional sharpening when problem is vague)
- *"How a bald-headed barber helped save my hair."* (verbal twist when direct solution is stale)

**Rule for Level 4:** headline starts with the *problem*, not the product, not the desire. Dramatize the problem before offering the fix.

---

## Level 5 — Completely Unaware (or Unwilling to Admit the Problem)

> "The prospect is either not aware of his desire or his need — or he won't honestly admit it to himself without being led into it by your ad — or the need is so general and amorphous that it resists being summed up in a single headline — or it's a secret that just can't be verbalized."
> — Schwartz, Ch. 2

**The hardest state.** The prospects are logically your market but psychologically "hundreds of miles away from accepting that product."

**Iron rules for writing to an Unaware market:**
1. **Price means nothing** — eliminate all mention of price.
2. **Product name means nothing** — keep product out of the headline.
3. **Direct claim of function/desire will not work** — do not mention product, price, function, or desire in the headline.

**What's left?** The **market itself**. You write an **identification headline**. You are calling your market together. You sell nothing, promise nothing, satisfy nothing — you echo an emotion, an attitude, a shared condition.

> "In this type of headline, you are telling them what they are. You are defining them for themselves. You are giving them the information they need and want, about a problem still so vague that you are the first to put it into words."
> — Schwartz, Ch. 2

**Worked examples from Schwartz:**

| Technique | Headline | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Give words to a hidden dream | *"The University of the Night"* (International Correspondence Schools) | Expanded a correspondence-school market beyond "earn more money" / "gain more skill" |
| Exploit a hidden fear | *"Why Men Crack…"* (Postum) | Revived a slipping coffee substitute by naming a fear the prospect hadn't consciously connected to coffee |
| Lead into an unacceptable problem via accepted image | *"Within the Curve of a Woman's Arm"* (Odorono deodorant) | Sold a taboo product by starting with a universally-accepted image |
| Project a desire that can't be said aloud | Marlboro Tattoo Ad — virile men, no words about virility | Sold cigarettes = virility without ever using the word |
| Use an unvoiced resentment | *"Why Haven't TV Owners Been Told These Facts?"* | Broadened a DIY market by exploiting resentment against service contracts |

**When Level 5 applies by default:** a product that has slipped out of public awareness, become "old-fashioned," or had its category exhausted. "Again, we are dealing with a matter of statistics. When a product begins to slip … when volume falls off … when the name of the product no longer sells as much … then that product needs to be reborn, and its problem is the problem of opening up an unaware market."

## Cross-cutting principle

> "Each of these stages is separated from the others by a psychological wall. On one side of that wall is indifference; on the other, intense interest. A headline that works wonders in the first stage will fail completely when addressed to a third-stage market where your prospect doesn't even realize that soaps can be made with built-in deodorants."
> — Schwartz, Ch. 2

**Headlines do not travel between awareness levels.** A Level 2 headline applied to a Level 4 market is noise. A Level 4 headline applied to a Level 1 market is waste.

## How to locate your market's awareness

Ask in order:

1. Does the prospect know my specific product exists, by name? **Yes → Level 1 or 2.**
   - Would they buy it today at a small discount? **Yes → Level 1.** Otherwise **Level 2.**
2. Does the prospect know they want what the product does? **Yes → Level 3** (if they don't know a product exists) or **Level 2** (if they know products exist but haven't picked yours).
3. Does the prospect recognize they have a problem/need the product solves? **Yes, but no named desire → Level 4.**
4. Would the prospect deny or fail to name the problem if asked directly? **→ Level 5.**

When in doubt, **locate the market one level LESS aware than you think.** Writing to a more-aware-than-actual market is the single most common failure mode. You end up talking about the product to people who don't yet care.
